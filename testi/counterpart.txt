We are used to thinking of empathy as something we feel toward the living:
a person, an animal, plants.
Mine, instead, goes toward things.

I walk, I look, and sometimes something inside a scene corresponds to me.
I don't know right away what it is, but I know it resembles me.

Originally, the word empathy meant exactly this:
what we feel before a form, an object, a landscape.
Only later did it pass to people.
Mine, then, is not an empathy that is missing.
It is the empathy of the past.

In these photographs the subject is not the thing itself, but the resemblance between me and it.
To find it, the image has to lose something.
The more it degrades, the more I can identify with it.
It stays real enough to still be a thing, and becomes abstract enough to take hold of me.
At that point I stop looking and begin to belong.
I let go of myself, and for a moment I am part of that fragment of the world.

Every image is a faceless self-portrait: not a place, but a part of me.

More than a project, it is an ongoing search born of a constant distress.
Merging with things consoles me.
The boundary between me and the space around me grows thin, without ever vanishing completely.
But each time, reality becomes ever less bearable.
