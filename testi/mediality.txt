Almost every gesture we make serves a purpose:
we pick up a glass to drink, we raise a hand to wave.
They have an intent, or a meaning others know how to read.

This project began by studying people living with Tourette syndrome.
Their bodies make sudden movements they do not fully choose:
gestures that grasp nothing, point to nothing, say nothing.
To our eyes they look ugly, wrong, almost non-human.
They are gestures without purpose, and that is precisely what makes them precious.

Photography can isolate a fragment of them and stop it.
Once stopped, the gesture ceases to look like a mistake to us and becomes something that deserves consideration.

The project focuses mainly on adolescents: as they grow up, these gestures tend to disappear.
Adolescence is their final season, an interrupted metamorphosis,
like Bernini’s Daphne, stopped in marble as she turns into a tree.

Even in neurotypical bodies, the gesture without purpose exists, though it is hidden:
it lives for an instant inside movements that do have a purpose.
Photography catches it and gives us back what we have unlearned how to see.

Our brain is a fragile organ:
it records the world we live in, with its fears and its rules, and turns us into what we are.
The more we manage to value a "useless" gesture,
the more the brain learns to free itself from what the world imposes on it.
In an age that measures every movement and demands that every gesture yield a return,
the gesture without purpose is the only one that escapes control, approaching a new form of freedom.

It also teaches us to look differently, empowering imagination over always seeking rational meaning.

(Samples from an ongoing body of work)
